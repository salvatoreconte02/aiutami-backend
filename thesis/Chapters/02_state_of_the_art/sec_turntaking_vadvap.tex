\section{Turn-Taking in Spoken Dialogue}
\label{sec:turntaking}

Turn-taking is the mechanism by which interlocutors coordinate who speaks when, and it is one of the basic structures of human conversation. A spoken dialogue system has to handle this coordination, but doing it computationally is hard, especially with more than two participants.

The reference theoretical model is the one proposed by Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson in 1974~\cite{sacks1974turntaking}. It defines \emph{Transition Relevance Places} (TRPs) as the points in an utterance where a change of speaker becomes appropriate, and gives three ordered rules for selecting the next speaker: the current speaker may select the next, a non-current participant may self-select, or the current speaker may continue. Heldner and Edlund~\cite{heldner2010pauses} later measured how efficient this system is in practice: inter-turn gaps in human conversation average around 200\,ms, which means listeners start planning their reply well before the current speaker is done. The design implication is direct: a system that waits for silence before processing input feels slow next to a human interlocutor.

Building on this theoretical base, the computational literature on turn-taking can be grouped into four families that mirror the broader evolution of speech technology. The first relies on fixed silence thresholds: when no speech energy is detected for 500--700\,ms, the system assumes the user has finished. This \emph{endpointing} approach is used by most commercial voice assistants such as Alexa and Siri. It is simple and robust, but it has a built-in trade-off: short thresholds clip the user during within-turn pauses (hesitations, breathing), long ones add perceptible latency. The second family adds linguistic information by looking at prosodic cues. Gravano and Hirschberg~\cite{gravano2011turncues} analysed the Columbia Games Corpus and identified six acoustic features (including falling pitch, final-syllable lengthening, and reduced intensity) that co-occur with turn boundaries; the more of them are present at once, the more likely the interlocutor will take the floor. Ward and Tsukahara~\cite{ward2000backchannel} similarly showed that low-pitch regions reliably predict good moments for backchannels. The third family applies machine learning to combinations of acoustic and linguistic features. Raux and Eskenazi~\cite{raux2009endpointing} trained a decision tree on prosodic, lexical, and dialogue-level cues in the CMU Let's Go bus information system, cutting both false endpoints and average response latency over a fixed-threshold baseline. Skantze~\cite{skantze2017turntaking} later reframed the task as a \emph{continuous prediction problem}: instead of binary decisions at single moments, a model continuously estimates the probability of yielding, backchanneling, or holding the turn, using LSTM-style recurrent networks. Meena et al.~\cite{meena2014turntaking} explored similar data-driven combinations of prosodic and contextual cues. Ekstedt and Skantze~\cite{ekstedt2020turngpt} pushed this further with \emph{TurnGPT}, a Transformer that predicts turn shifts from linguistic content alone, showing that a language model fine-tuned on dialogue data picks up turn-yielding patterns without seeing acoustic features. The fourth and most recent family uses large language models directly. Addlesee~\cite{addlesee2024ari} prompts an LLM to judge whether the user utterance is a complete conversational contribution before responding. Houde et al.~\cite{houde2025koala} have the LLM self-score its confidence that the user has finished, giving a soft and tunable endpointing signal. These methods benefit from the world knowledge of pre-trained models but pay for it in inference latency and rely on the LLM reasoning correctly about conversational structure. A review by Skantze~\cite{skantze2021review} covers all four families together with multi-modal cues, end-of-turn detection, and interruption handling.

Almost all of this work has been developed and evaluated in dyadic settings. Moving to more than two participants is not a smooth extension. The system has to decide not just \emph{whether} a transition is happening but \emph{who} should speak next. In face-to-face interaction, gaze direction is the primary signal for addressee selection and next-speaker nomination, but in voice-only or remote settings that channel is unreliable or absent. The system also has to deal with overlapping speech from multiple participants, speaker diarisation to attribute utterances correctly, and social dynamics such as schisms (parallel sub-conversations), dominance patterns, and unequal participation. Several turn-allocation mechanisms have been proposed. \emph{Explicit nomination} asks the current speaker to name the next one verbally. \emph{Gaze-based allocation} uses visual attention as an implicit signal: Johansson et al.~\cite{johansson2013gaze} showed that a robot interpreting and producing gaze cues can manage transitions more naturally. \emph{Self-selection races} follow the original Sacks et al.~\cite{sacks1974turntaking} model, where any participant may claim the floor at a TRP and the first to start typically wins. \emph{Moderator-mediated} allocation hands control to a designated party, human or artificial, that grants or denies speaking rights by some policy. On the modelling side, Bohus and Horvitz~\cite{bohus2009engagement,bohus2011multiparty} developed engagement models for multi-party interaction with embodied agents, covering engagement recognition, maintenance, and directed-action selection in scenarios with multiple co-present users.

No single mechanism has emerged as the consensus solution for voice-only multi-party settings. Gaze is not available, predictive acoustic models have not been shown to scale beyond two speakers, and LLM-based approaches add latency that conflicts with the 200\,ms gap budget. Explicit allocation protocols remain a pragmatic alternative widely used in computer-mediated conferencing systems, but the literature gives no settled answer.

\section{Voice Activity Detection and Projection}
\label{sec:vad-vap}

Voice Activity Detection (VAD) and Voice Activity Projection (VAP) sit at two different time horizons of speech analysis. VAD detects whether speech is happening now; VAP predicts whether it will happen in the near future. They are the low-level signal on top of which higher-level dialogue phenomena such as turn-taking, backchanneling, and barge-in detection are built.

VAD classifies, at each time step, whether speech is present in an audio signal. Early systems used hand-crafted acoustic features and lightweight statistical models; the WebRTC VAD is the best known example of this generation, offering low-latency frame-level decisions at low cost but with limited noise robustness. Modern neural VAD systems such as Silero VAD, the pyannote-audio pipeline, and Google's Personal VAD reach higher accuracy in noisy conditions while staying efficient enough for real-time use. In spoken dialogue, VAD has three roles: it \emph{gates} the ASR module so that only speech-containing segments are transcribed, it feeds the \emph{endpointing} signal that detects when a speaker has finished, and it supports \emph{barge-in detection} during system output. VAD detects only \emph{that} someone is speaking, not \emph{who} is speaking, which requires speaker diarisation, nor whether the detected speech is a complete turn or just a backchannel, which requires higher-level turn-taking prediction.

Voice Activity Projection (VAP), introduced by Ekstedt and Skantze~\cite{ekstedt2022vap}, is predictive rather than reactive: given the audio observed so far, the model forecasts the future voice activity of each participant over a horizon of 0.5 to 2 seconds. A dialogue system can use these predictions to anticipate turn transitions, backchannel opportunities, and overlap onsets before they happen. The VAP model takes stereo audio (one channel per speaker in a dyadic conversation) and extracts frame-level features with a self-supervised speech representation model such as CPC or wav2vec~2.0. A Transformer encoder then produces, at each time step, a probability distribution over the next voice activity states. The future is discretised into four temporal bins per speaker; with two speakers this yields $2^{4 \times 2} = 256$ possible state combinations. Training is self-supervised: the actual future voice activity observed in the data is the training signal, so no manual annotation of turn-taking events is needed. From this simple objective, complex turn-taking behaviours emerge implicitly: without being trained on turn-taking labels, the model learns to recognise turn-yielding cues, pick good backchannel moments, and tell turn-holds from turn-final silences. This suggests that much of the information needed for competent turn-taking is already in the acoustic signal and can be recovered by predicting a directly observable quantity. TurnGPT~\cite{ekstedt2020turngpt} had shown the same pattern in the text modality: a language model trained on dialogue transcripts learns turn-shift patterns from linguistic content alone.

The current VAP architecture is designed for dyadic interaction and assumes stereo audio with exactly two speaker channels. Extending it to multi-party settings runs into a scalability problem. With $N$ participants and four temporal bins per speaker, the number of possible future voice activity states grows exponentially as $2^{4N}$: for $N=5$ this is already over one million states ($2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$), which makes a direct extension of the output representation impractical. Processing all $\binom{N}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}$ pairwise speaker combinations independently does not scale either: it grows quadratically with $N$ and misses group-level dynamics that are not reducible to pairwise interactions. Malik, Atieh, and Skantze~\cite{malik2024vap} have named the multi-party extension of VAP as an open research problem and proposed initial strategies, including dimensionality reduction of the output space, hierarchical prediction, and attention-based architectures that can take a variable number of input channels. The problem is still open, with no established solution.
